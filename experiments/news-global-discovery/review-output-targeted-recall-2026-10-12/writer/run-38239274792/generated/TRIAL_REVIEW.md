Dr. Merrony, who heads the excavation, noted that this landscape contains the only Roman military site close to the location described in the Mabinogion, suggesting the presence of a heavily fortified Roman auxiliary fort may help resolve the mystery of the nearly 1,700-year-old legend.

### Intensive breathwork reduces brain blood flow by up to 45% and alters neural communication

Intensive breathing exercises can reduce blood flow to the brain by up to 45% and alter communication between brain networks, according to a brain-imaging study presented at the European College of Neuropsychopharmacology (ECNP) Congress in Munich. Researchers suggest that the intensity of the resulting altered states of consciousness may be linked more closely to changes in how brain networks communicate than to the reduction in blood flow alone. This finding challenges the common explanation that breathwork experiences are caused simply by the brain receiving less blood. During the study, 30 healthy, experienced breathwork practitioners performed high-ventilation breathing while undergoing MRI scanning. Brain imaging showed that global grey-matter cerebral blood flow fell by approximately 45% on average, dropping from 16.24 to 9.81 ml/100g/min during the session. Participants reported strong altered states of consciousness, describing a predominantly 'oceanic state'—a deep feeling of connection to the universe—that resembled the effects of a moderate to high dose of LSD or psilocybin, though with less pronounced visual effects. They also reported altered perception, vivid mental imagery, and changes in self-experience.

The study found that individuals who reported stronger altered states also showed greater changes in the brain's default mode network, a system of brain regions involved in self-reflection and self-related thinking. Dr. Christian Schmitz, lead researcher from the Central Institute of Mental Health in Mannheim, Germany, stated that while many practitioners report profound changes in perception, the underlying mechanisms have been poorly understood. He noted that these experiences appear linked to both marked changes in cerebral blood flow and alterations in how key brain networks communicate. Co-coordinator Hana Adolphi described breathwork as a legal, inexpensive, and non-pharmacological intervention that could serve as a tool for studying altered states of consciousness and informing future mental health treatments. However, she noted the study was relatively small and the findings are preliminary, requiring larger studies to determine clinical relevance. Dr. Guy Fincham of Brighton and Sussex Medical School, who was not involved in the study, noted the research was uncontrolled and used experienced practitioners. He suggested that replication in breathwork-naïve participants using a randomized-controlled design is necessary to determine if these findings translate more widely for therapeutic benefit. David Nutt, a professor at Imperial College London who was also not involved, described breathwork as a non-pharmacological bridge to research into psychedelics.
