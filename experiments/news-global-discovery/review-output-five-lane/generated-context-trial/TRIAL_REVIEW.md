University of Chicago scientists have described a well-preserved 165-million-year-old fossil of a mammal ancestor from China that shows early evidence of mammalian feeding and suckling traits. Measuring 40 to 50 centimeters long and weighing one to two kilograms, the creature possessed hind limbs similar to a platypus and a broad, flattened tail like a river otter, suggesting it was adapted for swimming and hunting in water. 

The fossil provides the earliest evidence yet of bony throat structures, specifically hyoid bones, which allow for modern mammalian feeding mechanisms. In modern mammals, hyoid bones are arranged in a U-shaped saddle suspended by bony segments to the skull, linking the mouth and throat passage to the esophagus and larynx. This apparatus enables adult mammals to use throat muscles to swallow chewed food one lump at a time rather than gulping whole prey, and it provides the ability for infants to suckle milk. 

Zhe-Xi Luo, the Mila Pierce-Rhoads Professor of Organismal Biology and Anatomy at the University of Chicago and senior author of the study published in *Nature*, stated that this innovation likely led to the diversification of mammal species into various dietary groups, such as carnivores, herbivores, and omnivores. While a smaller docodont named *Microdocodon gracilis* was discovered in 2019 with similar hyoid bones, the *Megacauda sungei* fossil offers better preservation of the delicate bony structures underneath the skull used for suspending palatal and pharyngeal muscles.
