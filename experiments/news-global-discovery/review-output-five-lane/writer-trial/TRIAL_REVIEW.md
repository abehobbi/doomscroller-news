### Astronomers identify host galaxy of 10.6 billion-year-old fast radio burst

Astronomers have identified the host galaxy of the most distant fast radio burst (FRB) recorded to date, a signal that originated approximately 10.6 billion years ago. In research published October 8, 2026, in the journal Science, a team led by Manisha Caleb of the University of Sydney announced they tracked the millisecond-long burst, designated FRB 20240304B, to a small galaxy containing roughly 10 million times the mass of the Sun. This host galaxy is significantly smaller than the Milky Way and appears to be a young, metal-poor galaxy actively forming stars, which differs from the larger galaxies where most other fast radio bursts have been located. The discovery indicates that these energetic radio emissions were being produced as early as 3 billion years after the Big Bang, a period characterized by intense star and galaxy formation.

The burst was initially detected on March 4, 2024, using the MeerKAT radio telescope in South Africa. Researchers utilized a system called MeerTRAP, designed to capture fast-changing transient radio signals in real time. To determine the distance, scientists analyzed the signal's dispersion, an effect where different radio frequencies arrive at slightly different times because the signal traveled through electrically charged material in space. While theories exist regarding what objects generate fast radio bursts, Manisha Caleb noted that conclusive proof of their origin remains unknown. Since their discovery in 2007, these enigmatic flashes have remained a mystery because most are observed only once.
